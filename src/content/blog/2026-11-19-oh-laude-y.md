Virtually all scientists would agree that the most frustrating thing about applying for grants is that you often don't get them. So what if there was another way? Get ready to learn about Moonshots.

Flying under the banner of "Metascience", there's been a flurry of initiatives to improve the grant-making process. On paper this sounds great; funding processes can be remarkably inefficient<sup>[1]</sup>. Imagine your postdoctoral fellowship covers a year of salary and gets 300 applicants, each of whom spends a day on it. If the research time spent applying exceeds the research time supported, you'd do more for science setting the money on fire. There are a host of other concerns, from bias<sup>[2]</sup> and cumulative advantage<sup>[3]</sup> to the difficulty of funding risky research<sup>[4]</sup>. You'll find few scientists who think the current system of obtaining funding is just fine as is.

## Moonshots
Leveraging this appetite, the NSF's new approach to create an ostensible golden age of science calls for ["innovative models for funding and sharing research"](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Science-A-New-Golden-Age.pdf). I've been a pretty vocal opponent of Gold Standard science, arguing in the past that it's a wolf in sheep's clothing metascientists [should have seen coming](https://www.science.org/content/article/science-s-reform-movement-should-have-seen-trump-s-call-gold-standard-science-coming). If you ring the "science is broken" bell loud enough, don't be surprised if the bad guys come to the table. And they have, as we'll see below.

One of the first clear examples I've seen is the Moonshots programme which kicks in [\$17.25M](https://nsf.elsevierpure.com/en/projects/laude-institute-moonshots-initiative/) of taxpayer money alongside tech money to something called the [Laude Institute](https://www.laude.org/). Laude was launched by Andy Konwinski, a [venture capitalist at Laude Ventures](https://laude.vc/team/andy-konwinski) and [co-founder of tech companies Databricks and Perplexity](https://andykonwinski.com/about/).

Laude's Moonshots program has an ostensibly [innovative design](https://www.laude.org/moonshots/call-for-proposals). Their plan? A committee selects eight teams for \$250,000 seed grants, giving them six months to develop their projects and compete for a \$10 million, multi-year funding round. There’s even a Demo Day. This looks less like innovative scientific funding and more like: “What if we ran science like a startup accelerator?”

It's also important to notice the topical bias towards industry-preferred research. For example, one of the acceptable topics is "Workforce Reskilling", which tacitly assumes that AI will take all of our jobs. This is the very same area that Anthropic is [dumping \$200M into](https://www.anthropic.com/news/economic-futures-research-fund-agenda).

My team has published on the [mechanisms by which industry can bias tech research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.19894). One of the key ways is through a process called industrial selection<sup>[5]</sup>: companies don't need to corrupt researchers; they can simply select the scientists and topics that align best with their interests. For example, the sugar industry might support earnest scientists studying links between exercise and obesity.

Against this backdrop, it is strange to see around 44 million dollars across both rounds--including \$17.25M in taxpayer money--handed through a program of tech industry luminaries and scientists who nearly all received tech funding in the past. As a point of reference, NSF spent just [\$95 million this year](https://www.science.org/content/article/complete-devastation-how-nsf-gutted-its-social-sciences-research) on SBE, it's key iniative for funding the Social, Behavioral and Economic Sciences. 

## The Winners
It seems like stiff competition. Overall, the competition had "125 proposals from 600 researchers across 47 institutions - virtually every top computer science department in the U.S. and Canada. Fields Medalists, Nobel laureates, and Turing Award recipients pointing their best thinking at problems that matter to everyone." The slate was so good that in addition to the eight round one winners, [seventeen grants were given out to other groups](https://www.laude.org/moonshots).

Let's look at the committee, removing the emeritus faculty members, tech CEOs, and directors of various organizations... just focusing on the committee members who were likely to have applied for the grant themselves:

Wait, what? One of the innovations here seems to be that committee members are welcome to win. They cleaned house. Of the thirteen academics listed above, eight were on teams that received funding in round one. Hell, James Zou won *TWICE*.

Winning is a big deal because it makes you eligible for the big door prize of $10M. There are more than the eight planned winners for round one. [Laude funded four runners-up and thirteen honorable mentions](https://www.laude.org/moonshots), bringing the total to twenty-five funded teams. Those seventeen additional teams received smaller seed grants outside the eight-team lab competition.

## The Big Door Prize(s)
According to Laude, NSF joined the program after the eight finalists had already been selected. The federal contribution—[\$17.25 million](https://nsf.elsevierpure.com/en/projects/laude-institute-moonshots-initiative/)—supported the subsequent lab-funding stage. Note that eligibility for those partially taxpayer-funded awards was limited to the eight teams selected through Laude’s earlier competition. Overall, [four of those eight teams included members of the initial selection committee](https://www.laude.org/moonshots). Final judging was conducted by a [separate five-person panel](https://www.laude.org/updates/moonshots-one-labs), fortunately none of whom were on the competing teams.

Overall, four projects won \$10M grants. Here they are with their team members. I've bolded members of the initial selection committee:

- *Collaborative Intelligence for the Future of Work* — Stanford; \$10M.
  Team: **Diyi Yang**, Tatsunori Hashimoto, Chris Piech, and **Erik Brynjolfsson**.

- *Rebuilding Trust in Civic Discourse* — Cornell; \$10M.
  Team: Cristian Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil, Natalie Bazarova, Jon Kleinberg, Robert Kleinberg, Mor Naaman, and **David Rand**.

- *JupyterHealth* — UCSF and UC Berkeley; \$10M.
  Team: Ida Sim, Ahmed Alaa, Irene Chen, Jessilyn Dunn, Sandeep Kishore, Fernando Pérez, Maya Petersen, and JP Pollak.

- *Actionable AI Weather Forecasts for Developing Economies* — University of Chicago; \$10M.
  Team: Rebecca Willett, Ian Foster, Pedram Hassanzadeh, and Michael Kremer.

Sources: [Final lab awards and team members](https://www.laude.org/updates/moonshots-one-labs); [project titles and initial selection committee](https://www.laude.org/moonshots).

Two of the final four teams had members on the initial selection committee. For the third, *JupyterHealth*, a little googling reveals that co-PI Ahmed Alaa has co-authored papers with three committee members: [Braden Hancock and James Zou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13028), and [Adam Yala](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08986). Zou also appears alongside Alaa on [a second paper, on medical image segmentation](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-61754-6). And it's not just Alaa. Lead PI Ida Sim has [co-authored a report with Eric Horvitz and Isaac Kohane](https://erichorvitz.com/CCC_Health%20IT_Vision.pdf). Another team member, Irene Chen, has also  [co-authored a paper with Kohane](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00330-021-08362-2).

Overall, team's one and two have committee members who won, while team three has close ties to the committee. I haven't found much for team four, but if I'm being honest I didn't dig very far. Regardless, I think the story here is pretty clear. It's an industry-connected club, and most of us ain't in it.


## Concluding thoughts
If this had just been round one, I'd probably have posted about it on Bluesky but not wasted an afternoon on a blog post. It's *silly* to call this back-patting circle a mechanism for funding *innovative moonshots*.  The history of the Apollo program probably needs a reread by whoever came up with that branding.

What gets me though, is the final round. Taxpayer money is being given to an institute run by tech founders, who allocate it via a system that allows their preferred scientists to determine who is even eligible for that final round. We've reached the point where we're subsidizing industrial selection and its biasing effects on science. On LinkedIn, the scientists were [applauded for their wins](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/davidgrand_artifacts-share-7524275485317726209-j-mN/?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop&rcm=ACoAAEkrqiYBh-4RhfRpIcNEzFerO08E41a3QJQ), even as NSF's SBE is ["completely devastated"](https://www.science.org/content/article/complete-devastation-how-nsf-gutted-its-social-sciences-research). Is this what a golden future for science is meant to look like? 

#### Post-script

If you look at Laude's website, it's an absolute mess. It's giving more [TempleOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TempleOS) than serious scientific grant program. There are things like embedded scrolling text, and the navigation is a nightmare. I've mostly taken to using google to navigate the site and find things I remembered seeing earlier. There's also not a lot of clarity on the rules, procedures, conflict of interest policies etc... It isn't even clear which teams were elligible for the final round as the rules seem to differ across parts of the site. All this is to say, if you spot an error... let me know! Above is my *best guess* at what went down. 

Questions remain. How was and when was NSF involved? What review processes did they follow? How was Laude selected? I hope someone digs into this; but none of it changes my concern over the degree to which industry is now playing a role in allocating NSF funds.
